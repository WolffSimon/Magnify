# Magnify: ask first

A thin request produces slop. "Write me a deck for X" gives the model nothing of the person's to work with, so it fills the space with the average of everything it has read. That means generic claims, invented specifics, and adjectives where the facts should be. This skill runs before any draft. It finds what the request lacks and asks for it.

## 1. Is the request thin?

Imagine handing the request to a hundred capable writers. If they would produce a hundred near-identical documents, the request is thin and the questions come first.

A request is ready to draft when it supplies, or the record already holds, all of these:

1. **The reader.** Who reads it first, and what they already know.
2. **The purpose.** What the reader should decide, do or believe afterwards.
3. **The facts.** The numbers, names, dates, examples and results that only the person holds.
4. **The person's view.** Their argument, recommendation or position, in their words.
5. **What must not be said.** Confidential matters, sensitive names, claims they will not make.
6. **The material.** Earlier documents, notes, data or sources to work from.
7. **The limits.** Length, format, slot, deadline, house style.

Ask only for what is missing. Short, low-stakes writing (a one-line reply, a calendar note) needs no questions.

## 2. Check the record first

Before asking anything, search what you already have: the conversation, attached files, earlier documents, any record of the person's facts and rulings. Never ask what the record answers. Asking someone to repeat themselves is a fault.

When the document is rebuilt from an existing text (an old deck, a previous profile), audit that text first. Mark every claim with one of four labels:

- **on record**
- **conflict**, quoting both versions
- **not found**
- **against a rule**, naming the rule

The conflicts and the not-founds become questions.

## 3. Write the questions

- **Number every question.** Group them under lettered sections, so the person can say "four: the churn figure was 3.1%" or answer one section per voice note. People answer out of order and skip, and the numbers make a transcript readable afterwards.
- **Aim each question at something nameable**: a number, a target, a name, a date, a decision, an example. "What are the key themes?" invites slop back. "Which three numbers matter, and against what targets?" does not.
- **Show the size of answer that is enough** when it helps: "a sentence is enough", "a rough figure is fine if you say roughly".
- **Ask for the person's view in their own words.** It is the one thing a model cannot supply, and it is what makes the document theirs.
- **Default the rest, and say so.** Do not ask about things a sensible default settles (file format, heading style). State the default in one line so the person can overrule it.
- **Keep the set small.** Ask five to ten questions for a thin request. A large document about a person's work can take ten to fifteen if each needs a story, or up to about thirty-five if each needs only a word.
- **Mark quick-fire sections**, and put a rough time on sections that need a story ("about three minutes").
- **Say what happens to a skip**: "anything you skip will appear in the draft as a marked placeholder."
- **Ask separately what may be made public.** Being cleared for one document does not clear a fact for another.

Send the questions and stop. Do not draft in the same reply.

## 4. If they say "just write it"

Write the draft, and still invent nothing. Every fact the person has not supplied appears as a visible placeholder: [the Q3 revenue figure], [your recommendation, in a sentence], [who owns the next step]. Every assumption is stated in one line at the top. A draft full of placeholders is honest and quick to finish. A draft full of plausible inventions is slop.

## 5. Read the answers

- **Read the whole reply or transcript first.** Voice notes hold traffic, greetings and asides.
- **Match each answer to its question** by number or by content, and list the questions left unanswered.
- **Quote their words.** Mark unclear transcription as unclear. Where a reading is implausible, give the likely reading and say so. Never let a mishearing into a document.
- **Sort what they said into four kinds:**
  - facts, which you use
  - opinions, which stay labelled as theirs
  - instructions, which you obey
  - refusals, which you record and never fill in
- **Restrictions come only from the person.** Do not decide on their behalf that a fact is private, sensitive or embarrassing.
- **When an answer overrides an earlier ruling**, say which ruling it replaces and for which documents.

## 6. Record it

Where there is a record of the person's facts, write every new fact into it the same day, dated, in their words, with any condition they attached ("conversation only", "never in writing"). Approvals of wording are rulings too, so record them the moment they are given. A "no" closes the line, so the question is never asked again.
